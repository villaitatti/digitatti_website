And those tools often start with digitization, which is a critical component of much of our work.

We build our own mass digitization systems, designed to be low-cost and to maximize the volume of material we can process. Our systems handle the whole pipeline. We often start from the structure of the archive, its boxes and the folders inside them, with a minimal amount of metadata; the software then guides the scanning operator, who places each item, a letter, a photograph, a document, a 35-millimetre slide, on the scanner, and moves on.

We then use AI to transcribe the contents, write a caption and assign categories, producing the kind of structured data we can then use to publish that material.

The transcription is the most important part, because it feeds the next step, enrichment. If a photograph of an artwork says it is held at the Metropolitan Museum, we normalize the name, look up where the museum is, and place it on the map; if it names an artist, we match the artist to reference vocabularies, which give us a standard name, when they were born and died, and where they were active. This reduces our need to catalogue everything by hand, but in many cases we still need to verify connections where there is some ambiguity.

From there we publish the collections on platforms anyone can explore,

as well as provide tools so that our catalogers and researchers can augment and repurpose that data in ways that respond to their own research interests.

And all of this is with and for scholars: they bring their own sources into our tools, gather them in one place, and analyze them as a whole.

As part of the digital humanities side of our work, we also actively contribute to the field of computer science, because many of the technical issues we work on are highly applicable to the hard sciences as well. One could even argue that, because of the complexity and uncertainty of humanities and historical data, some of the technical requirements we face are actually more challenging than in other scientific disciplines.

Our earlier work focused primarily on semantic web and linked data technologies. On one side, we use rich semantic structures to describe relationships between people, places, events and objects; on the other, linked data connects us to large shared authorities on the web, such as the Getty's Union List of Artist Names, Wikidata, VIAF and so on. In this way we can disambiguate between artists, places, repositories and artworks.

We have also done a lot of work on visual similarity: systems that let people find similar artworks and recognize the same artwork across different collections, allowing us to connect what researchers in different places have said about a work.

We also do extensive benchmarking of AI tools: measuring their capabilities, finding their shortcomings, and seeing where AI can help on projects and take over some of the tedious work. This ranges from transcribing early modern and contemporary handwriting, to Renaissance music notation, to image captioning.

We are also working to bridge the usability gap of semantic web technologies with AI. Semantic web systems are precise, but to search them you have to write queries in a specialist query language that very few historians know. So we build systems that use the natural-language abilities of AI to translate a research question, asked in plain words, into the language of the semantic web, and to bring the answer back. And as a side note: Remo is currently at the International Semantic Web Conference, where he just won the best paper prize for precisely this work.

Finally, we work on question-answering systems for large image collections: the ability to retrieve images based on what they actually show, rather than on their data, and to answer art-historical questions based on these collections.

introductionthe challenge

One of our challenges: much of the humanities' historical data is hard for digital research to reach.

Before we can map, search or analyze anything, we have to get to it, and in a form a computer can read.

In books

Catalogues, lists and inventories, printed as prose, often still in copyright: rich data that no computer can read as it stands.

In archives

Photographs, letters and diaries: often not digitized, or scanned but never transcribed, so they cannot be searched.

Behind paywalls

The secondary literature that explains these sources is licensed for reading, rarely for analysis at scale.

In footnotes

Years of research end in a book; the evidence is cited at the bottom of the page, not available as data.

In closed databases

Scholars' personal archives and project databases, in closed formats, offline once the grant or the career ends.

Most scholars in the humanities work alone. One of our goals is to open up access to these sources, connect them, and let people build on each other's work.

One of the biggest challenges in the digital humanities is access. Much of the historical data needed to support humanities scholarship is still locked away: under copyright in books, undigitized in archives, behind a paywall, in footnotes; or, even when it is in digital form, it is often in a scholar's personal archive, on their own computer.

One of our main goals is to open up access to these sources, connect them, and let people build on each other's work. For the rest of this talk, we will try to show you our approach to tackling this challenge.

Maps lend themselves particularly well to digital analysis. Most historical data is text; maps belong to a small subset of historical data that is computationally actionable from the start: a place with coordinates on a map, with a related date.

Now think of a map in a printed book. A line shows the movement of people or objects across a region. This is the challenge Metapolis addresses. It grew out of exactly this situation, in the lab's Venice project, Archipelago: in 2020 the sources lived in one database and the maps in another.

Think of the three phases of a research project on a place. They may be done by three people, or by the same person. First, collecting the sources: the historian documents them in a database of documents, people and events. Second, drawing the buildings on a map, phase by phase. Third, writing the story: a book.

Each result is valuable, and each is incomplete on its own. The database sits on one computer, often in a closed format, and the documents can never be shown next to the buildings they describe. The map has shapes with nothing behind them: who built this, when, how do we know? And the book is read once, front to back; nobody can search it, ask it a new question, or add to it.

What is missing is a shared workspace: one map that moves through time, where every building is one click from its sources, and every contribution is credited to the person who made it.

That is the challenge Metapolis addresses. And it is not theory: this is exactly how our own Venice project, Archipelago, started. In 2020, the sources lived in one database and the maps in another, and nothing joined them.

How does a building get onto the map? Four steps. First, a historical map, for example the Austrian land survey of Venice of 1838 to 1841, is georeferenced: laid exactly over a modern base map, so that every point on it has real-world coordinates.

Second, we retrace the buildings from that historical map in a GIS: each building becomes a polygon, a shape with coordinates, with the years it stood.

Third, we describe it: we connect the documents, the owners, the builders, the events, each with its own source.

Fourth, we connect it: we cite the source and link to libraries, archives and museums that know more about the same people and places. The moment it is saved, it is on the platform, credited to whoever entered it. No programmer has to be in the loop.

Our second city: Florence. Florentia Illustrata started in 2018 as a partnership of scholars working on the city. Its backbone is the 1835 land survey of Florence, the Catasto Generale Toscano: the first precise survey of the whole city, every plot of land, its owners, and what stood on it. Once the survey is connected data, you can ask it questions it was never designed to answer. Here is one our team asked: how many women owned property in Florence in 1835? The survey does not record gender at all: owners are listed by name, father's name, title.

So we followed the clues. An owner described as “moglie di”, wife of someone, is a woman. Some first names are always female. Some owners are widows, some are nuns.

The result is a new layer on the map: parcels owned by women alone, shared, by men alone, or by institutions. This is a paper by Erica Andreose, a former intern of ours, with Remo and Gianmarco, presented this year. These are estimates, not certainties, and the paper says so.

What did women own? Among parcels held by individuals, about one in ten belonged to women, and that share is roughly the same in every category. In numbers, mostly homes: about 1,100, against some 9,200 held by men. A gender history of property, from a source that never mentions gender, without changing the data at all.

The scanning station at I Tatti: off-the-shelf scanners, and our own software, which reads a barcode on the back and files both sides in the right folder.

With the digitization, our goal was not just to scan photographs, but to create a digital twin of the archive: every photograph, front and back, recorded in its exact location, the box, the folder, even its order within the folder, so that the archive can be explored online exactly as Berenson arranged it. What you see is a time-lapse of the scanning station: off-the-shelf Epson scanners that provide FADGI 4-star reproduction, two to three at a time, and our own software (this one here is a much older version), which reads a barcode on the back of each photograph, crops and straightens, and files both sides automatically.

For most of the scholars who need this archive, Florence is on the other side of the world. With a digital twin, the entire collection can be consulted remotely.

And this archive holds historical data found nowhere else. On the backs of these photographs, Berenson, his staff and the photographers recorded attributions and changes of mind, who owned a picture and when, sales, condition; and for many works that have since been damaged, destroyed or lost, these photographs are the best record that survives. Put together, this information contributes to a body of knowledge about these works that museums and scholars can build on.

The value of this archive is as much on the back as on the front. So we sent each photograph, front and back together, to an AI model, which returns a structured record: artist, title, place, date, materials, inscriptions, stamps, owners and exhibitions.

Our first batch, in June 2025, covered 112,000 photographs using the Gemini 2.5 Pro model. The models have improved so quickly that we have since redone the transcription with Claude Opus 5.5, and so far we have not found a single mistake. Benchmarking it against GPT Astra 6, there were only minor differences between them, and those differences were only in how each structured the data; neither was objectively wrong.

The collection is scheduled to be made public in February, once our catalogers complete a manual review, grouping multiple photographs of the same artwork into a single record.

Because AI runs through many of our processes, we did want to mention how we use it with caution.

One of our guiding principles is that the source decides, whether the material is processed in some way by AI tools or by scholars. One of the great advantages of working in the digital humanities is that we can easily link to the original source material. Every statement is tied to the page, letter or photograph it came from, so any reader can check it.

Two: we measure before we rely. We build test sets with known answers, compare several AI systems, and publish the numbers, including when AI is not good enough.

Three: uncertainty stays visible. Illegible words are flagged, approximate dates stay approximate, and when sources conflict, both are kept.

Four: people make the critical judgments. A person reviews every edge case that may require more complex reasoning.

Five: we preserve. For many of our past projects, the websites we linked to went offline: for an older project from 2017, 97 percent of the links were dead after five years. We now use Harvard's Perma.cc service to web-archive the pages we cite, more than 21,000 so far.

Strand two: what they studied. Berenson's Lists are his catalogues of which Italian Renaissance painter made which picture, and where each picture could be found. For much of the twentieth century they were the reference: they shaped attributions, museum labels and the art market. Until now they existed only as heavy printed volumes.

Today the final editions, Venetian 1957, Florentine 1963, Central and North Italian 1968, are a searchable edition: 4,312 pages, 506 artists, 21,829 works, each tied to the printed page it came from. AI read every page directly from the image, and the results were checked against the books' own logic: plate numbers must run without gaps, artists must be in alphabetical order, the city indexes must agree. Then the ambitious part: about sixteen thousand works, three quarters, are now linked to their records in museums today. The rule is strict: a shared name or number is never enough; 875 coincidental matches were rejected.

For Kansas City: the Lists record 32 works here, by 27 artists, and almost all are now linked to their current records, most at the Nelson-Atkins.
