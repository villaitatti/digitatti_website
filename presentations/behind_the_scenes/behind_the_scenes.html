Catasto Generale Toscano, 1835 · as modelled in Florentia Illustrata.
Start with a crowd: the register of the 1835 Florentine cadastre scrolls by, real names, one after another, parcel after parcel. In a list they mean little. Then it slows down and stops on one of them. Start with a person. In 1835 the Florentine cadastre lists a shop in Piazza S. Andrea, in the Old Market, owned by Rosa Morelli. The register does not describe her on her own terms: it calls her "moglie di Jacopo Mosel", wife of Jacopo Mosel. Yet her name leads to six properties: three shops, one of them a silversmith's, and three buildings. And the place itself is gone: the Old Market was demolished at the end of the nineteenth century to make room for today's Piazza della Repubblica. Press → four times. The point: archives hold thousands of lives like hers, but they are scattered across records that nobody has connected. That is what this work is about.
why it matters · 02the problem

History survives in pieces.

scattered

Every archive, library and museum keeps its own piece of the story, in its own catalogue.

connected by hand

Scholars spend years linking the pieces, and the links end up in footnotes.

The evidence about a city, a building or a person is never in one place. A deed is in a state archive, a book in a library, a drawing in a museum, a photograph in a photo archive, and the connections between them often only in a scholar's own notes. Each institution catalogues its own holdings, in its own way. Researchers spend years connecting the pieces by hand, and when the book is published, those connections end up in footnotes, where nobody else can build on them.

Bringing the pieces together.

Today the pieces sit apart, each in its own archive.Connect: records from different archives point to the same people, buildings and events.Research together: scholars with different skills build on the same evidence.Tell stories: a narrative walks through the evidence, and every step leads back to a source.
The same archives as before. Press →: we connect them. A deed, a book, a drawing, a photograph, a note: once they point to the same person, the same building, the same event, the pieces start to talk to each other. Press →: scholars join, a historian, an architect, a cartographer, each adding what they know to the same shared evidence, and each credited for it. Press →: on top of this, we can tell stories, narratives that walk through the evidence step by step, where every sentence can be checked against its source.
why it matters · 04the aim

Three things we want to make possible.

01 · connect
Archives that talk to each other

Records kept in different places, linked to the same people, buildings and events.

02 · research together
One shared body of evidence

Scholars in different places and fields build on each other's work, and every contribution is credited.

03 · tell stories
Stories you can check

Narratives built on data curated by specialists: every claim one click from its source, doubts stated, open to new questions.

So the aim is threefold. Connect: let records held in different archives speak to each other, through the people, buildings and events they share. Research together: give a community of scholars, in different places and fields, one shared body of evidence, where each contribution is credited. Tell stories: let them publish narratives built on critically curated data, where every statement can be traced back to its source, uncertainties are stated, and readers can ask new questions of the same evidence. why it matters · 05an illustrative story · step through with →

A place is a network, through time.

 
An illustrative story, built one event at a time. On a map, a building is just a shape: that is what our tools see. Then its history: actors on the left (who), events in the middle on the building's life line (what happened, and when), sources on the right (how we know). Arrows go from the actors to the event they took part in, with what they did, and from each source to the event it documents. 1460, a wool merchant commissions the building, and a building contract documents it. 1590, his heirs sell it to a noble family: a notarial deed. 1720, an architect adds a new wing: his drawing survives. 1890, the City demolishes the wing for a new street: a photograph shows it. Press → again: a clock runs down the building's life, the footprint changes with each phase, and what has not happened yet is faded. why it matters · 07how Archipelago and VeNiss started · step through with →

Three experts, three outputs, one connected result.

Many projects start like this: three experts, each with an output of their own.Each output is good at one thing, and misses the rest.Some questions can only be answered across the three outputs.Our aim: keep the three outputs connected, in one shared workspace.
Archipelago, 2020: 131 sources, 147 events and 174 people in a database; the geometries in a separate map; the narrative in a text file.
Three people, each doing good research, each producing an output. The historian collects sources in a database; the mapmaker, a GIS specialist, draws the geometries in a desktop GIS; the author writes the narrative for print. This is literally how Archipelago and then VeNiss started: a database for the sources, a GIS for the geometries, and nothing linking the two. Press →: what each output misses. We spent years experimenting with these tools, from desktop GIS to digital exhibits and story-map tools: each solves part of the problem, none covers the whole research cycle, from collecting sources, to drawing, to interpreting, to publishing something others can search and reinterpret. Press →: the questions that only the three outputs together can answer. You could not show a document next to the building it describes, or link a change in the urban fabric to the event and the people who caused it. Press → again: our aim, the three outputs kept connected in one shared workspace.

Today, years of research usually end as a printed book or a flat dataset. The sources are buried in footnotes, and the reader cannot go back to the document. The map is the author's view, printed once. Readers cannot ask a different question without starting over, and the data stays locked in personal files and formats that age. The most valuable part of the work, the evidence, becomes secondary. What we want instead is a living resource: every claim one click from its source, a map that moves through time, open to new questions by place and by time, and data that others can reuse for decades.

Metapolis is a map-centric research and publication platform that links archival, bibliographic and multimedia sources to interpretation, on a map that moves through time, designed for non-technical users. metapolis · 02step through with →

Extend, don't rebuild.

The obvious idea: build a new map editor in the browser. It would take years, and never be as good.Instead: keep QGIS, and transform ResearchSpace from within, with maps, a timeline, new forms and stories, linked live to GIS. That is Metapolis.A footprint drawn in QGIS lands on the web map, linked to its people, events and sources, in its own years.
Drawing and georeferencing stay in QGIS, free mapping software used by mapmakers worldwide. Data, enrichment, search, authoring and publishing come from ResearchSpace, an open-source research platform created at the British Museum. On the right you can see what ResearchSpace lacks: a map. Step 0: the obvious idea was to rebuild a map editor in the browser. We decided early not to: it would take years and never be as good as QGIS. Press →: instead, Metapolis transforms ResearchSpace from within: everything in crimson on the right is Metapolis, the map and its timeline, new forms, relations that carry notes, stories, together with the live link to the geometries drawn in GIS. Press →: a footprint drawn in QGIS travels across the bridge and lands on the web map, where it is linked to the people, events and sources of the knowledge graph, with a timeline that shows it in its own years.
metapolis · 03the whole process · step through with →

From historical evidence to the web map.

 
Finda historical map
Drawa footprint
Connectlive, no export
Enrichdata from elsewhere
Publishon the web map
no export step

Edit the drawing, refresh the web map.

another backend

Geometries stored inside the graph itself, as for Florentia Illustrata.

The whole process, one step per →. Find: it starts from evidence, a historical map, georeferenced so that it lines up with today's streets. Draw: a GIS specialist traces the building's footprint over it in QGIS and gives it start and end years. Connect: the geometry is saved in a spatial database, PostGIS, where GIS people already work; ResearchSpace queries it live, through SQL Sail, so nothing is exported, and in the knowledge graph the footprint becomes a Building. Enrich: historians link it to people, events and documents, and to data from elsewhere, such as archives, Wikidata, VIAF, WorldCat and the Getty vocabularies. Publish: on the web map the building appears in its years, one click away from its evidence. An alternative geometry backend: QLever, or any GeoSPARQL endpoint; Florentia Illustrata reads its parcels from QLever.

metapolis · 05the data model · step through with →

Relations are entities too.

four entities

Building · Event · Person · Source, each with its own form.

linked

Every link has a meaning: took place at, owned, documents.

relations are entities

With domain, range and notes: not only that a source is connected, but why.

enriched

A person in our graph is the same as on Wikipedia and Wikidata; also VIAF, WorldCat and the Getty vocabularies.

an example

A house in Via dell'Amorino, acquired by Fulvia Targioni Tozzetti, documented by the 1835 cadastre.

Four core entities: Building, Event, Person, Source. Relations are entities too, with domain, range and notes. Enrichment from Wikipedia and Wikidata, VIAF, WorldCat and the Getty vocabularies: at step three, a person in the graph, here Filippo Brunelleschi as a well-known example, is linked to his Wikidata identity, Q174330, and through it to his Wikipedia page. One design choice to highlight: a relation is not just a line between two things. It is an entity with its own notes, so a scholar can record why a source is connected to a building, not only that it is. That is where interpretation lives. Press → four times; the last step gives a concrete case from Florentia Illustrata: a house in Via dell'Amorino, the acquisition of title, Fulvia Targioni Tozzetti, and the 1835 cadastre.

metapolis · 08built to last

Software is ephemeral. Research data should not be.

RDF · Linked Open Data · the ontology travels with the data · open source: github.com/villaitatti/metapolis
Data stored as RDF, Linked Open Data; meaning travels with the data, because the ontology is inside the model; open-source code on GitHub. Software is ephemeral; research data should not be. Because the data is RDF with its semantics embedded, it can be read and moved to another system decades from now, without Metapolis.

florentia illustrata · 03a research question

Rosa Morelli was not alone. How many women owned property in 1835 Florence, and what did they own?

A question the cadastre was never meant to answer · Andreose, Grillo, Spinaci, AIUCD 2026
Back to Rosa Morelli, the shop owner we met at the start. Once the cadastre is a graph, her record is not an isolated curiosity: we can ask how many women owned property in 1835 Florence, and what kind of property. It is a question the cadastre was never designed to answer: it does not even record gender. This is the research question of the AIUCD 2026 paper by Erica Andreose, Remo Grillo and Gianmarco Spinaci. The next slides show how we got there, what we found, and where it is told: in a story on the platform.

the paper

Andreose, Grillo, Spinaci, “Beyond Spatial Data”, AIUCD 2026.

Because the data is a graph, it can answer questions it was not built for. The cadastre does not record gender. But by following the "moglie di", wife of, relation, and by matching first names against an open list, a team identified women landowners, including widows and nuns, and mapped them: 79 more likely unmarried women, 3 of them nuns. That is the AIUCD 2026 paper Beyond Spatial Data, by Erica Andreose, Remo Grillo and Gianmarco Spinaci: a gender history of property from a cadastral dataset, without changing the data model.

VeNiss, Venice's Nissology: Reframing the Lagoon City as an Archipelago, a model for spatial and temporal urban analysis, 16th to 21st centuries. An ERC Starting Grant (2023–2027), PI Ludovica Galeazzo, based at the University of Padua, Department of Cultural Heritage, with the Department of Architecture of the University of Florence and I Tatti. The project was conceived in I Tatti's Digital Humanities Lab. It studies Venice's more than sixty "domestic" islands, places for food supply, defence, healthcare and civic rituals, from the Cinquecento to today. VeNiss is the project whose needs shaped Metapolis.

Where VeNiss started and where it is. In 2020 the Archipelago FileMaker database held 131 sources, 147 events, 174 people and 57 places. Today the VeNiss graph counts about 7,500 entries: 1,351 primary and 512 secondary sources, 690 events, 589 people, 59 islands and 262 buildings, 1,698 archival units, 2,203 images, 27 georeferenced historical maps. The narratives turn the data into arguments. The Lagoon and Public Health follows how isolation made the islands the place for quarantine: the Senate decreed the Lazzaretto Vecchio in 1423, enlarged in 1429; in 1468 the Lazzaretto Nuovo, on the island of Vigna Murata, for quarantine, to prevent contagion; centuries later, in 1759, it was declared unusable. Other narratives look at the islands and the city's food supply, and at public and private spaces. Each statement in the story is one click away from the map and from the sources. Press → four times. (The dates come from VeNiss records still in progress.)
