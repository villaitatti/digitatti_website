why we need it

why · 01step through with →

A place is a network, through time.

A place is not just its shape. It is a network: a building linked to people, events and sources, and the links change over time. Press → four times: people, events (a patron commissions, a family enlarges, owners demolish: events tie people to the building), sources, then the clock runs through the years: people come and go, events happen, sources accumulate, the network and the footprint change. Spoken line: "A city is not a set of shapes on a map… our tools mostly see only the shapes."

why · 03how Archipelago and VeNiss started · step through with →

Three people, three outputs, no connection.

Archipelago, 2020: 131 sources, 147 events and 174 people in a database; the geometries in a separate map; the narrative in a text file.
Three people, each doing good research, each producing an output. The historian collects sources in a database; the GIS specialist draws geometries in a desktop GIS; the author writes the narrative for print. Each output is part of the research, and nothing connects them. This is literally how Archipelago and then VeNiss started: a database for the sources, a GIS for the geometries, and nothing linking the two. You could not show a document next to the building it describes, or link a change in the urban fabric to the event and the people who caused it. Press → three times: each attempted link breaks.

The cost for scholarship: years of research end as a print book or a flat dataset. Sources are buried in footnotes; readers cannot search by place and time; data is locked in silos and proprietary formats. The most valuable part of the work, the evidence, becomes secondary. The reader sees the final map, but cannot go back to the document, cannot ask a different question, and cannot reuse the data.

metapolis

NEH Digital Humanities Advancement Grant · built on an open-source research platform
metapolis · 01in one sentence

A map-centric research and publication platform.

one place

Archival, bibliographic and multimedia sources, linked to interpretation, on a map that moves through time.

for scholars

Designed for non-technical users.

veniss.net · the platform at work
Metapolis is a map-centric research and publication platform that links archival, bibliographic and multimedia sources to interpretation, on a map that moves through time, designed for non-technical users. Two words matter here: research and publication. Metapolis is where you do the work and where you show it: the same data, no export step in between.
metapolis · 02step through with →

Extend, don't rebuild.

The platform is open source: created at the British Museum, now maintained by its team and community.
Drawing and georeferencing stay in the geographical tools (QGIS). Data, enrichment, search, authoring and publishing come from a platform, ResearchSpace, open source, created at the British Museum. Metapolis adds the geospatial layer that joins them. We decided early not to rebuild QGIS in a browser: it would never be as good. Instead we built on ResearchSpace, which already handles knowledge graphs, forms, search and publishing, and added what was missing: maps, geometries, time, and the bridge to GIS.
metapolis · 03architecture

Drawn once, live on the web map.

Exploreon the map
no export step

Edit the drawing, refresh the web map.

another backend

Geometries stored inside the graph itself, as for Florentia Illustrata.

Geometries stay in PostGIS, where GIS people already work. ResearchSpace queries them live, through SQL Sail, so a polygon drawn in QGIS appears on the web map without any export. Everything else, who, what, when, which source, lives in the knowledge graph as Linked Open Data. An alternative geometry backend: QLever, or any GeoSPARQL endpoint; Florentia Illustrata reads its parcels from QLever.

metapolis · 05the data model · step through with →

Relations are entities too.

four entities

Building · Event · Person · Source, each with its own form.

linked

Every link has a meaning: took place at, owned, documents.

relations are entities

With domain, range and notes: not only that a source is connected, but why.

enriched

From VIAF, Wikidata, WorldCat and the Getty vocabularies.

an example

A house in Via dell'Amorino, acquired by Fulvia Targioni Tozzetti, documented by the 1835 cadastre.

Four core entities: Building, Event, Person, Source. Relations are entities too, with domain, range and notes. Enrichment from VIAF, Wikidata, WorldCat and the Getty vocabularies. One design choice to highlight: a relation is not just a line between two things. It is an entity with its own notes, so a scholar can record why a source is connected to a building, not only that it is. That is where interpretation lives. Press → four times; the last step gives a concrete case from Florentia Illustrata: a house in Via dell'Amorino, the acquisition of title, Fulvia Targioni Tozzetti, and the 1835 cadastre.
metapolisthe map, in four moves

A familiar map, with time and evidence built in.

veniss.net ·
The heart of it: a familiar, Google-Maps-like interface, but with time as a first-class dimension and every feature one click away from its evidence. Four moves, recorded on VeNiss: compare superimposed historical maps; visualize past and present; highlight places by metadata, year by year; navigate the documents from the buildings. Clips play in order; click one to jump. The live demos come with the use cases.

VeNiss: a primary source described next to its image, a bird's-eye view of the island of La Certosa (c. 1650), in the IIIF viewer.
Archival images served via IIIF; transcription through web forms; contributions attributed to each researcher; narratives annotated with links to entities. The document is not an attachment at the end of the book. It is on the map, next to the building it describes, and it can be read, transcribed and cited from there.
metapolis · 08built to last

Software is ephemeral. Research data should not be.

RDF · Linked Open Data · the ontology travels with the data · open source: github.com/villaitatti/metapolis
Data stored as RDF, Linked Open Data; meaning travels with the data, because the ontology is inside the model; open-source code on GitHub. Software is ephemeral; research data should not be. Because the data is RDF with its semantics embedded, it can be read and moved to another system decades from now, without Metapolis.

The cadastre records no gender. The graph can find it.

Homonyms and identities are not resolved yet.

the paper

Andreose, Grillo, Spinaci, “Beyond Spatial Data”, AIUCD 2026.

Because the data is a graph, it can answer questions it was not built for. The cadastre does not record gender. But by following the "moglie di", wife of, relation, and by matching first names against an open list, a team identified women landowners, including widows and nuns, and mapped them: 79 more likely unmarried women, 3 of them nuns. That is the AIUCD 2026 paper Beyond Spatial Data, by Erica Andreose, Remo Grillo and Gianmarco Spinaci: a gender history of property from a cadastral dataset, without changing the data model.

veniss.eu · the platform: veniss.net
VeNiss, Venice's Nissology: Reframing the Lagoon City as an Archipelago, a model for spatial and temporal urban analysis, 16th to 21st centuries. An ERC Starting Grant (2023–2027), PI Ludovica Galeazzo, based at the University of Padua, Department of Cultural Heritage, with the Department of Architecture of the University of Florence and I Tatti. The project was conceived in I Tatti's Digital Humanities Lab. It studies Venice's more than sixty "domestic" islands, places for food supply, defence, healthcare and civic rituals, from the Cinquecento to today. VeNiss is the project whose needs shaped Metapolis.

The narratives turn the data into arguments. The Lagoon and Public Health follows how isolation made the islands the place for quarantine: the Senate decreed the Lazzaretto Vecchio in 1423, enlarged in 1429; in 1468 the Lazzaretto Nuovo, on the island of Vigna Murata, for quarantine, to prevent contagion; centuries later, in 1759, it was declared unusable. Other narratives look at the islands and the city's food supply, and at public and private spaces. Each statement in the story is one click away from the map and from the sources. Press → four times. (The dates come from VeNiss records still in progress.)
